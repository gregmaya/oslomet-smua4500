The Reality

  • Complexity: Cities are not closed systems, unlike factories.
  • Wicked Problems: Moving a variable in a city (e.g., traffic) affects everything else (pollution, retail, housing).

Indicators & Proxies

The Map is not the Territory

The Trap of Abstraction


The “Index” Problem

  • Liveability Index: 85/100
  • Happiness Score: 7.2
  • Walkability Score: 94

Why it fails

Mathematically sound, practically useless.

  • If the score drops to 82, do we fix potholes or add schools?

Abstract indexes hide the levers of change.

  • Explain the difference between an Indicator (raw, valid signal) and an Index (aggregated, abstract score).
  • Ideally, we want indicators that point to specific physical actions.
  • An “Index” is good for ranking cities in a magazine, but bad for running a city.

The Trap of Abstraction


“The map is not the territory.”

— Alfred Korzybski

  • The Origin: Alfred Korzybski was a scientist and philosopher who wanted to remind us that our description of reality is never the same as reality itself.
  • Defining the Map: In our context, the “Map” is the Digital Twin, the GIS layers, the census data, and the AI’s predictive model. It is an abstraction.
  • The Value of Omission: A map is only useful because it leaves things out. If a map of Oslo was as detailed as Oslo itself, it would be a 1:1 scale and completely useless.
  • The Danger for Planners: The “Trap” occurs when we start making decisions based only on the map’s logic, forgetting the territory’s complexity.
  • Example: A transport model (The Map) says a new highway will reduce commute times. But the territory (The People) responds with “induced demand,” filling the road immediately. The map failed to capture human behavior.
  • AI and “Hallucinations”: When an AI gives a confident but wrong answer, it is “believing its own map.” It is calculating the probability of words or pixels, not the physics of the street.
  • The Planner’s Duty: Our job is not just to build better maps; it is to be the person who constantly walks the territory to see what the map missed.
  • Question for the class: “If the Digital Twin shows a neighborhood is ‘optimally served’ by transit, but the residents feel isolated, which one is ‘true’?”

The Unintended Consequences

Optimizing for the wrong thing

The Metric

“Average Vehicle Speed” or “Intersection Throughput”

Optimizing for flow efficiency.

The Result

Stroads & Highways

  • High speed = Low interaction.
  • A street with “perfect flow” has no life (commerce, lingering, playing).

Goodhart’s Law

“When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.”

  • Example of Traffic vs Place.
  • If you optimize for one variable (speed), you inadvertently kill another (place quality).
  • This is why choosing what to measure is a political act, not just technical.

The “Misleading” Interface

The Aesthetic Trap

“If it looks high-tech, it must be true.”

  • Shiny 3D maps and “Minority Report” interfaces can mask poor data quality.
  • Cognitive Bias: We trust beautiful things more than ugly truths.

The Black Box

  • False Precision: Displaying “Safety: 87.42%” implies a level of certainty that doesn’t exist.
  • Hidden Algorithms: Who decided the weightings?
  • Truncated Realities: What is not lying on the dashboard? (e.g., happiness, social capital).
  • Connect this to your Viz Pitfalls: “Misleading graphs, improper scaling.”
  • In Smart Cities, there is a risk of “Technological Solutionism.”
  • As curators of these tools, the students must ensure the visualization doesn’t lie about the model’s uncertainty.

Choosing the Right Chart

NYTimes Visual Vocabulary

What NOT to do

“Mistakes, we’ve drawn a few.”

The Don’ts

  • Truncated Axes: Starting a bar chart at 50% to exaggerate a 1% difference.
  • Spaghetti Charts: Too many lines making trends unreadable.
  • Correlated Scales: Forcing two unrelated variables onto dual axes to imply causation.

The Wrong Type

  • Using a Line Chart for categorical data (implies continuity where there is none).
  • Using a Pie Chart for 20 categories.
  • Honesty in Data Viz is crucial for Decision Support.
  • If you truncate an axis, you might convince a Mayor to spend millions on a problem that is actually a minor fluctuation.
