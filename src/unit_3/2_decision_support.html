GOAL

To reduce the “Cognitive Load”

without oversimplifying the “Wicked” reality.

  • Remind them of the “Brain” analogy. A brain is useless if it can’t move the body.
  • Decision Support Tools are the nervous system. They take the data from our models and present it so a Mayor, a Planner, or a Citizen can say “If we do X, then Y happens.”
  • Crucial point: A good DST doesn’t hide complexity; it manages it.

History & Evolution

From the Cockpit to the City

Origins in Business Intelligence

The Corporate “Cockpit”

  • KPI Culture: “If you can’t measure it, you can’t manage it.” (Peter Drucker).
  • Performance Monitoring: Dashboards were designed for CEOs to track sales, inventory, and efficiency in real-time.
  • The Metaphor: The city as a machine that can be “tuned” for efficiency.
  • Start with the roots. Dashboards didn’t start in cities; they started in factories and boardrooms.
  • The goal was efficiency and profit maximization.
  • Ask: “Does a city work like a factory?” (Hint: No).

The Shift to Urban Planning

Importing the Logic

The Promise

  • Smart Cities (2010s): Tech giants (IBM, Cisco) sold the idea of the “City Operating System.”
  • Real-time feedback: Moving from 5-year census data to millisecond sensor readings.

The Reality

  • Complexity: Cities are not closed systems.
  • Wicked Problems: Moving a variable in a city (e.g., traffic) affects everything else (pollution, retail, housing).
  • Transition: When we brought these tools to cities, we brought the “efficiency” mindset with them.
  • Challenge: Cities are open, complex systems, not closed production lines.
  • This creates tension between “optimizing flow” and “creating place.”

From Monitoring to Simulating

The modern leap

1. Monitoring

“What is happening now?” - Traffic counters - Air quality sensors - Energy grids

2. Analyzing

“Why is it happening?” - Correlating data - Finding patterns - Identifying bottlenecks

3. Simulating

“What if we change X?” - Digital Twins - Impact assessment - Decision Support!

  • This is the key evolution. We refrain from just looking at the speedometer.
  • We are now building “Flight Simulators” where we can crash the plane (city) safely in digital space before making changes in physical space.
  • This is the definition of a Decision Support Tool.

Indicators & Proxies

The Map is not the Territory

The Trap of Abstraction

“The map is not the territory.”

— Alfred Korzybski

The “Index” Problem

  • Liveability Index: 85/100
  • Happiness Score: 7.2
  • Walkability Score: 94

Why it fails

  • Mathematically sound, practically useless.
  • If the score drops to 82, do we fix potholes or add schools?
  • Abstract indexes hide the levers of change.
  • Explain the difference between an Indicator (raw, valid signal) and an Index (aggregated, abstract score).
  • Ideally, we want indicators that point to specific physical actions.
  • An “Index” is good for ranking cities in a magazine, but bad for running a city.

The Unintended Consequences

Optimizing for the wrong thing

The Metric

“Average Vehicle Speed” or “Intersection Throughput”

Optimizing for flow efficiency.

The Result

Stroads & Highways

  • High speed = Low interaction.
  • A street with “perfect flow” has no life (commerce, lingering, playing).

Goodhart’s Law

“When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.”

  • Example of Traffic vs Place.
  • If you optimize for one variable (speed), you inadvertently kill another (place quality).
  • This is why choosing what to measure is a political act, not just technical.

Good vs. Bad Indicators

Feature Good Indicator Bad Indicator
Actionable “Potholes per km” (We can fill them) “Road Quality Index” (Vague)
Relevant Measures what matters to stakeholders Measures what is easy to count
Comparable Standardized units (kg, m, $) Arbitrary scales (1-5 stars)
Transparent Clear calculation method “Black Box” algorithm
  • Go through the criteria.
  • Emphasize “Proxy”: Sometimes we can’t measure the thing (e.g., Safety), so we measure a proxy (e.g., Streetlight lux levels).
  • Always ask: Is the proxy actually correlated?

Anatomy of a Dashboard

1. Indicators

The “Vital Signs” - Real-time feeds - Historical trends - Key Performance Indicators (KPIs)

2. Simulation

The “What-If” Engine - Slider controls - Scenario toggles - Impact forecasting

3. Narrative

The Context - Annotations - Storytelling flow - “So what?” (Insights)

  • Refer to your Data Viz notes: “Identifying patterns, trends, and outliers.”
  • In an urban context, a dashboard shouldn’t just be a “report card” of the past. It should be a “flight simulator” for the future.
  • Example: A dashboard that shows current CO2 levels is a viz; a dashboard that lets you “delete” a parking lot and see the CO2 drop in real-time is a Decision Support Tool.

The “Misleading” Interface

The Aesthetic Trap

“If it looks high-tech, it must be true.”

  • Shiny 3D maps and “Minority Report” interfaces can mask poor data quality.
  • Cognitive Bias: We trust beautiful things more than ugly truths.

The Black Box

  • False Precision: Displaying “Safety: 87.42%” implies a level of certainty that doesn’t exist.
  • Hidden Algorithms: Who decided the weightings?
  • Truncated Realities: What is not lying on the dashboard? (e.g., happiness, social capital).
  • Connect this to your Viz Pitfalls: “Misleading graphs, improper scaling.”
  • In Smart Cities, there is a risk of “Technological Solutionism.”
  • As curators of these tools, the students must ensure the visualization doesn’t lie about the model’s uncertainty.
